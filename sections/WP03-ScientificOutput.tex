\subparagraph{Work Package 3 - Access to RIs for Accelerators} \mbox{}
\label{sec:wp3_scientific_output}

The scientific projects of the WP3 TA users spanned a wide range of research frontiers, exploiting the unique capabilities of the facilities. Most of the scientific output is described in Deliverables D3.1--D3.4; selected highlights, including experiments carried out after these deliverables were completed, are presented below.

\textbf{\textit{Laboratory Astrophysics}} \mbox{}

During the final period of the project (P3), \textbf{HRMT-68} and \textbf{HRMT-76} experiments were conducted at the HiRadMat facility at CERN, building on the previous work in \textbf{HRMT-62} and \textbf{HRMT-64}. \textbf{HRMT-68} aimed at understanding long scale-length beam-plasma interactions and identifying plasma instabilities. The high-intensity proton beam enabled the generation of plasma conditions that cannot be reproduced elsewhere. The following experiment, \textbf{HRMT-76}, focused on validating the observation of particle acceleration by self-modulated beam instabilities. A limited amount of changes were needed to adapt the setup of \textbf{HRMT-68}, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:FBIV_FRMT76}.

The experiment concluded the data taking on week 24, 2026. The data analysis is ongoing, with high impact publications being prepared as well as 1 Diploma thesis (V. Tsouknida, NTUA)

\textbf{\textit{Material R\&D}} \mbox{}

\textbf{HRMT-63} experiment, carried out in collaboration between CERN and J-PARC, aimed to measure the displacement cross section for 440 GeV/c protons to improve the assessment of radiation-induced damage in accelerator materials (see Fig.~\ref{fig:DPA_HRMT63}). The measurements provide valuable experimental data for validating DPA models and improving predictions for components exposed to high-intensity proton beams. Post-irradiation analyses have provided benchmark data to validate radiation-damage simulations and improve the accuracy of DPA calculations.

\textbf{HRMT-67} was conducted in collaboration with Fermilab and built on previous RaDIATE experiment campaigns. The scope of the experiment incorporated advancing material thermal shock studies and deepening the response of both conventional and novel materials used in beam-intercepting devices. A distinctive aspect of the experiment is the investigation of whether beam-induced stress intensities can reach levels sufficient to trigger unstable crack propagation in conventional materials used for targets and beam windows. The preliminary results of \textbf{HRMT-67} established baseline responses using low-bunch beam exposures, allowing beam-induced frequencies on the materials to be characterized without inducing crack extension. The radiation mapping results are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:radiate_HRMT67}.

\textbf{HRMT-74} experiment focused on the irradiation and performance evaluation of 10 novel materials intended for use as Beam TV (BTV) screens in high-brightness accelerator environments. The campaign is being carried out within the framework of a dedicated PhD thesis (P. Alexaki). The light yield produced by the irradiated samples (see Fig.~\ref{fig:HRMT74}) is currently under detailed analysis, while scanning electron microscopy (SEM) investigations are planned in the coming months to assess potential structural modifications and radiation-induced damage. This study is of high strategic importance for future high-brightness machines, as threshold knowledge of radiation tolerance, optical response, and material stability is essential for the development of next-generation beam diagnostic systems.
Identifying materials capable of withstanding extreme beam conditions while maintaining reliable optical performance is critical for machine protection, beam monitoring, and operational efficiency.

In the framework of this project, a master student was supported by EURO-LABS to prepare and implement the beam-based alignment (BBA) procedure required for the HRMT-74 experiment at the HiRadMat facility. The work focused on rebuilding the BBA acquisition-and-analysis tool used to determine the beam position relative to the target with sub-millimetre precision. The BBA procedure consists of sweeping the target or intercepting block across the beam and fitting the resulting beam-loss edges to determine the beam centre in the target reference frame.
The work included the development of a stable and maintainable architecture for the BBA application, replacing the previous tool, as well as the integration of live control-system readout through JAPC, including beam-loss acquisition, stage position and motor status, manual stage movement, and emergency-stop functionality. The rebuilt application was deployed at the CERN’s Control Center (CCC) console and validated against offline data using a real stage. A layered, thread-safe architecture separating the analysis, controller, and graphical user interface was implemented, addressing the stability issues of the previous BBA application, which could crash when control actions were performed during a scan. The new application is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:BBA_HRMT74}.

\textbf{\textit{Accelerator Instrumentation R\&D}} \mbox{}

\textbf{HRMT-71} followed-up experiment \textbf{HRMT-55} and was conducted in collaboration with CERN and ESS. The experiment focused on assessing the performance of several Beam Loss Monitor (BLM) technologies under representative high-intensity SPS beam conditions. The campaign focused specifically on ionisation chambers (ICs) and lower-sensitivity ionisation chambers (LICs), which play an important role in ensuring system integrity. Their response, linearity, dynamic range, and robustness were evaluated under controlled beam-loss conditions to improve beam-loss detection for machine protection and diagnostics.
The results of the experiment will provide benchmark data for comparing and calibrating the different BLM technologies. The measurements will support improved signal interpretation and detector deployment strategies, contributing to the optimisation of BLM systems for 
HiRadMat, the CERN accelerator complex, and future high-brightness accelerator facilities. First results for the LIC detectors are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:HRMT_71}.

\textbf{R\&D for future accelerators - I} \mbox{}

The MACHAFILM platform at CEA-IRFU/Synergium provides state-of-the-art niobium (Nb) surface treatments (electrochemical polishing and thermal annealing in vacuum) on SRF cavities and Quadrupole Resonators (QPR) to achieve the best possible baseline prior to thin film deposition. During the reference period, tests were conducted on samples provided by several laboratories, including HZB, FNAL, CERN, JLAB, STFC, and INFN. In particular, HZB supplied two Nb QPRs initially to IJClab/SUPRATECH, where they underwent mechanical polishing before being transferred to Synergium for electropolishing, vacuum thermal treatment, and a second electropolishing step. The treated samples were then returned to HZB, where RF tests demonstrated outstanding baseline performance, establishing reliable references for future high-Tc thin film depositions. Over the last months four additional QPRs from HZB and University of Hamburg have been processed at CEA and IJClab. Additionally, the MACHAFILM platform was solicited by STFC to perform electrochemical and thermal treatments, as well as cleanroom assembly, on three bulk Nb cavities to achieve optimal RF baseline performances for future high-Tc thin film deposition.

The development of next-generation SRF cavities relies on new superconducting thin films and multilayers capable of operating at higher temperatures (up to \SI{4.2}{K}) while maintaining the performance levels currently achieved with bulk Nb at \SI{2}{K}. Within the European particle accelerator roadmap and the IFAST, ISAS and EPITA projects, high-Tc superconducting films are planned for deposition on Cu or Nb cavities and QPRs, with RF performance assessments at cryogenic temperatures. In this regard, the growth of high-quality high-Tc superconducting alloys such as NbTiN and Nb3Sn is crucial, as they possess critical temperatures roughly twice that of Nb. Achieving higher operating temperatures would significantly reduce cryogenic costs, aligning with key goals of the European high-energy physics strategy.

Beyond surface treatments, the MACHAFILM platform has also been active in characterizing superconducting films. Several laboratories including FNAL, CERN, JLAB, STFC, and INFN provided NbTiN and Nb3Sn films deposited on Nb, Cu, and sapphire substrates. These samples were analyzed using tunneling spectroscopy (TS) and X-ray diffraction (XRD) at MACHAFILM (see Fig~\ref{fig:Nb3Sn_STFC}). TS, in particular, has proven highly predictive for RF performance by assessing film quality at the nanoscale. More than eight samples were evaluated, and detailed reports summarizing the results were sent to the respective institutions.
\begin{figure}[!htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{graphics/Nb3Sn-STFC.png}
    \caption{(A) summary of the TS results obatined on the \ce{Nb3Sn}/Cu samples from STFC. (B) Comparison of the \ce{Nb3Sn} films chemical composition from different institute. }
    \label{fig:Nb3Sn_STFC}

The Nb SRF cavities can also be used as Qubits and to developed new surface treatments to suppress defects in the oxide layers know as two level systems (TLS). These defects are known to destroy coherence and lower the quality factor in the quantum regime. On the MACHAFILM platform CEA pioneered a new approach based on ALD and post annealing treatments to mitigate TLS defects and increase by an order of magnitude the quality factor of Nb cavities at low accelerating field, were TLS dominate.  In this context the University of Hamburg and DESY solicited the MACHAFILM platform to conduct RF tests at low accelerating gradients on an ALD coated and annealed Nb SRF cavity.



\textbf{\textit{R\&D for future accelerators - II}} \mbox{}

% --- from EURO-LABS Newsletter No. 6 (September 2026), pp. 8-9: M. Himmerlich, V. Pathania (CERN)
\textbf{Photoelectron yield of accelerator vacuum material surfaces}\mbox{} 

For the current upgrade of the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) to its high-luminosity (HL-LHC) configuration, as well as for the technology of a potential Future Circular Collider (FCC) at CERN, electron emission processes in the beam pipe play an important role, since they create operational limitations for the particle accelerators\footnote{L.A.~Gonz\'alez \textit{et al.}, Phys.\ Rev.\ Acc.\ Beams \textbf{24} (2021) 113201.}. Technology development for particle accelerators therefore requires profound material studies. For the design and performance of their vacuum systems, photon-, ion- and electron-induced processes at the material surfaces are of great relevance and must be known. The related material properties are often only accessible through dedicated experiments. The Beam Screen Test Experiment (BESTEX) at the Karlsruhe Research Accelerator (KARA) was established some years ago for photon-stimulated desorption measurements of vacuum systems\footnote{L.~Mether \textit{et al.}, ``The electron cloud challenge for the HL-LHC'', 42$^{\mathrm{nd}}$ Int.\ Conf.\ on High Energy Physics, 2024.}.

Secondary electron yield (SEY) measurements can easily be implemented for material studies on a laboratory scale, while photoelectron yield (PEY) experiments often require an adequate light source, i.e.\ synchrotron light of the right spectral range. To address the needs of PEY studies for material and coating developments, the BESTEX beam line was recently upgraded. The major modifications were the introduction of four electrically isolated test samples that can be independently biased and directly benchmarked against each other, and the truncation of the synchrotron light using vertical and horizontal apertures.

The first commissioning and test run (02/2026--08/2026) was performed to validate the photoelectron emission reduction of copper surfaces using functional carbon coatings. Thin layers of amorphous carbon are applied to critical vacuum components of the HL-LHC in the form of 100~nm thick films for components of up to several metres in length\footnote{B.~Henrist \textit{et al.}, ``Large scale production of amorphous carbon coatings for the new beam screens of the HL-LHC project'', Proc.\ IPAC'26, MOP7175.} and in the form of only 15~nm thin films for several 53~m long half-cells in the arcs of the LHC\footnote{V.~Petit \textit{et al.}, ``Amorphous Carbon Thin Films for Electron Cloud Mitigation in the LHC Arcs: Developments Towards In-situ Implementation'', Proc.\ IPAC'26, MOP7106.}. Figure~\ref{fig:kit_pey_setup} shows how the individually biased test samples were integrated using ceramic spacers. Each specimen can be characterised sequentially in the experiment using the synchrotron radiation of KARA. Degreased copper surfaces correspond to the original surface state when the LHC was built, while the carbon coatings of different thickness not only change the visual appearance of the material but also significantly vary the electron emissivity of the surface when exposed to UV light.

    \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{graphics/BESTEX_PEY_samples_photo.png}
    \caption{Top: schematic of the electrically isolated test samples mounted for the first run of PEY experiments at BESTEX, comparing different carbon coatings to bare Cu surfaces. Bottom: photograph of the 200~mm $\times$ 20~mm samples prior to their integration into the system. (Courtesy: M.~Himmerlich, V.~Pathania, CERN)}
    \label{fig:kit_pey_setup}

The experiment was designed to allow a quantitative assessment of the PEY based on calculations with SynRad+, a synchrotron radiation simulation tool. Furthermore, a direct comparison of the electron emissivity of the different surfaces is possible, and its variation can be characterised during continuous irradiation of the surfaces with synchrotron radiation, which typically induces transformation processes at the outermost surface of the material.

Figure~\ref{fig:kit_pey_results} highlights the differences in the PEY of the various surfaces. The most important result is the confirmation of the electron emissivity reduction, which can reach a factor of more than 2. This may not sound significant, but it has an immense influence on the global generation of electrons during operation of the LHC. Moreover, the measurements confirmed a reduction of $\sim$30\% for a carbon coating only 15~nm thin. These experimental results contribute to the material parameter database for the simulation of LHC operating conditions in the HL-LHC era and confirm the applicability of the current upgrade campaigns at CERN, which coat critical vacuum components of the accelerators with amorphous carbon thin films. The first PEY test campaign was very successful and benefited from the support of the KIT experts on vacuum, alignment and operations at KARA, as well as from EURO-LABS support.

\textbf{Application-oriented R\& D}

% --- from EURO-LABS Newsletter No. 6 (September 2026): A. Gilardi on behalf of the CLEAR team (CERN)
\textbf{CLEAR: experiments, infrastructure and training at a flexible electron test facility} \mbox{} 

The CERN Linear Electron Accelerator for Research (CLEAR) has been one of the most versatile facilities accessible through EURO-LABS. It runs as an independent accelerator, which means it can keep delivering beam to users even when the rest of the CERN complex is shut down---a decisive advantage as the Long Shutdown 3 phase approaches. In a typical year the facility operates for 30 to 40 weeks, from March through December.

Two sections of roughly 20~m each provide electrons from 20 to 220~MeV, with bunch charges between 0.01 and 1.5~nC, trains of 1 to 150 micro-bunches spaced at 1.5 or 3.0~GHz, and repetition rates from 0.833 to 10~Hz. Four test areas, two in air and two in vacuum, sit alongside the VESPER, EOS and plasma lens installations. Together with a small hands-on team, this layout lets a user go from an idea to a beam test remarkably quickly.

The breadth of the user community reflects that flexibility. Medical applications accounted for 43\% of the experiments carried out over the project, ahead of machine development (17\%) and instrumentation (16\%), with machine learning, plasma, impedance, detector and radiation-to-electronics studies making up the remainder. Roughly two thirds of the requests came from outside CERN: 38\% from universities and 31\% from other research institutes.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{graphics/WP3-CLEARsecondline.jpg}
    \caption{The CLEAR beam line in the CERN East Area. The new beam line built with EURO-LABS support reached 92\% transport on its first commissioning run. (Courtesy: A.~Gilardi, CERN)}
    \label{fig:clear_beamline}
\end{figure}

Much of the medical programme concerns Very High Energy Electron (VHEE) radiotherapy. A dual-scattering system developed at CLEAR produces a flat, super-Gaussian transverse dose distribution over a centimetre-scale field, replacing the peaked Gaussian profile that had previously limited irradiation studies; it is now used routinely in CLEAR operation. In parallel, a silica optical fibre array read out by a CMOS camera was developed as a beam profile and dose monitor able to cope with the ultra-high dose rates at which conventional ionisation chambers saturate. These tools underpinned a study of FLASH irradiation effects in zebrafish embryos published in \textit{Radiotherapy and Oncology}, which showed that changing the picosecond microstructure of the beam alters morphogenesis without affecting hydrogen peroxide production.

Furthermore, two complementary machine-learning activities were pursued. The first treats about 60 logged variables---RF, magnets, laser, ambient conditions and charge---as a multivariate time series and asks whether one quantity can be inferred from the others. Evaluated at the current time step, this gives a virtual diagnostic; projected forward, it becomes a fast and adaptive stabilisation tool. The second applies reinforcement learning to beam steering: after a few hours of training, the agent corrects the trajectory across three consecutive screens in a single step, and autonomous steering has now been demonstrated on the machine (Fig.~\ref{fig:clear_rl_steering}).

\begin{figure}[!htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{graphics/CLEAR_RL_beam_steering.png}
    \caption{Autonomous beam steering with reinforcement learning at CLEAR: beam images on three consecutive screens before (top) and after (bottom) correction. (Courtesy: A.~Gilardi, CERN)}
    \label{fig:clear_rl_steering}

EURO-LABS funding also went into hardware. A new beam line was designed, installed and commissioned, reaching 92\% transport on first operation and confirming that the line is well conceived and well aligned (Fig.~\ref{fig:clear_beamline}). It serves two new user stands: an in-air stand with a large-diameter final chamber, and a 1.7~m in-vacuum stand. Diagnostics improved in step, with screens now resolving features of about 3~$\mu$m thanks to a dedicated camera, a cooling plate and 3D-printed tungsten shielding. An in-house 3D printer has become part of the standard workflow, producing holders, shields and mounts on demand, and a structured Bessel laser beam was introduced as a novel alignment solution.

CLEAR also hosted three editions of the EURO-LABS Advanced Training School on Operation of Accelerators, in 2024, 2025 and 2026. Early-career participants worked directly on the machine, alongside the PS Booster, ISOLDE, AD/ELENA and LEIR, combining lectures on control systems and beam dynamics with hands-on shifts in the control room.

CLEAR is scheduled to operate at least until 2030, with no long shutdown currently foreseen. Several of the developments seeded by EURO-LABS have already become operational tools, and the facility remains open to new proposals from anyone with a strong idea to test.
