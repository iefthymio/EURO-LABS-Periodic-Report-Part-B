\subparagraph{Task 2.1: Stable Ion Beams} \mbox{}


At \textbf{GANIL}, the experimental programme in P3 spans from nuclear physics and astrophysics to topics in the interdisciplinary research program. The most important projects concentrated on the nuclear moment studied of short-lived excited states, competition between fission modes in pre-actinides, measurement of the $^{12}$C Hoyle state radius via double-excitation inelastic scattering, production of $^{18}$F in novae, radiation damage in binary oxides, or exploring the molecular growth in ionic form of heteromolecular clusters.
An example of a highlight can be seen in the experiment to study the Hoyle state.
The detailed properties of the $^{12}$C second 0$^{+}$ excited state, known as the Hoyle state, are a challenge for nuclear structure theory and have a key role in the synthesis of the elements. The experiment performed at GANIL (see Fig.~\ref{fig:WP2_GANIL_Fazia}) aimed at measuring the mean matter radius of this state by analyzing the diffraction structure of single-and double-excitation in $^{12}$C+$^{12}$C inelastic scattering at 105 MeV beam energy. 
The experimental setup consisted in the coupling of FAZIA and INDRA multi detectors. Few nuclear excited states have attracted the interest of the scientific community as much as the 12C Hoyle state (second 0$^{+}$ state at 7.65 MeV excitation energy), which is remarkable in many respects: aside from its key role in the synthesis of $^{12}$C, its description still remains a challenge for nuclear structure theory. In nuclear structure, the Hoyle state is crucial to understand clustering in nuclei. Theoretical calculations show different hypotheses regarding its spatial configuration but most of them predict a strongly developed 3 alphas structure and a matter radius significantly larger than the $^{12}$C ground state. In recent years, many experimental efforts have been made to characterize the Hoyle state decay properties and the rotational band built on top of it. However, only a few attempts to access experimentally the Hoyle state radius have been made, mostly using inelastic scattering of electrons, protons and light nuclei (up to $^{12}$C) on $^{12}$C target. The FAZIA –INDRA collaboration measured the Hoyle state radius by comparing single-and double-excitation in $^{12}$C + $^{12}$C inelastic scattering. This experiment supported by EURO-LABS is opening a fantastic opportunity for the collaboration with the use of FAZIA a mult-particle correlator at very forward angles and INDRA as a nearly 4$\pi$ gamma calorimeter using the CsI scintillator for spectroscopy of cluster, near-threshold calorimetry as a bridge between nuclear dynamic and nuclear structure.

At \textbf{ALTO} facility the TREFLE project was conducted in P3. It concerns the study of heavy-ion induced two-nucleon transfer reactions, aiming at offering additional constraints in the calculation of the nuclear matrix elements (NMEs) of the neutrinoless double $\beta$ (0$\nu$$\beta$$\beta$) decay. This idea is based on a pioneer theoretical work in which the NMEs for a variety of nuclei candidates for the (0$\nu$$\beta$$\beta$) decay were decomposed in terms of the intermediate nucleus states with two less nucleons and it was found that the contribution to the value of the NMEs is exhausted by 0$^{+}$ ground state (g.s.) with smaller contributions coming from other excited states. This interesting result suggests that one may provide rigorous experimental constraints in the calculation of the NMEs of (0$\nu$$\beta$$\beta$) decay though the study of two-particle transfer reactions. To this date, the results of this work have not been experimentally validated. As such, it was conducted the study of the complete network of two-nucleon transfer reactions in the $^{12}$C+$^{76}$Ge and $^{12}$C+$^{76}$Se collisions at the energy of 85 MeV, exploiting for the first time the possibility to offer additional experimental constraints in the calculation of the NMEs of the (0$\nu$$\beta$$\beta$) decay. 

At \textbf{GSI}, currently a topic of investigation across the community, Multi Nucleon Transfer (MNT) reactions at the Coulomb barrier offer a promising route to produce unknown neutron-rich nuclei and possibly access the super heavy mass region. A new method combining a high-resolution TOF spectrometer (TOSCA) with a linear micro-calorimeter (measuring kinetic energy) was tested. Precise $\Delta$E measurements behind a homogeneous solid absorber allow complete A and Z identification. 

Continuing the high profile and high impact measurements of the structure of heavy elements, laser spectroscopy studies of californium, fermium, nobelium, and lawrencium aim to probe nuclear structure near N=152 and Z=100. The established RADRIS technique and the high-resolution JetRIS method were used to expand level searches and investigate ground and isomeric states, such as the K-isomer in $^{254}$No thereby refining modern nuclear models. 
A significant milestone for astrophysics has been reached at the CRYRING@ESR storage ring (cf. \url{https://doi.org/10.1016/j.physletb.2016.10.015}).: for the first time, nuclear reactions were measured at energies that mirror conditions inside stars. The $^{15}$N(p,p)$^{15}$N and 
$^{15}$N(p,$\alpha$)$^{12}$C reactions were investigated in inverse 
kinematics using the CARME array (see Fig.~\ref{fig:WP2_GSI_CARME}), reaching down to 426 keV/u (E$_{cm}$ = 403 keV) — by far the lowest energy at which a 
nuclear reaction has been measured in a heavy-ion storage ring. This new experimental approach opens the path toward studying how the elements in the universe were formed with even greater precision.

At the \textbf{IFIN-HH Tandem Accelerator Complex}, one series of experiments exploring the shape coexistence in Sn and Cd nuclei can be highlighted here. 
Extensive studies on shape coexistence were pursued at IFIN-HH in the years of 2021-2024 by the Milano (Milano Univ. and INFN) – Krakow (IFJ PAN) - Bucharest (IFIN-HH) – Bruxelles (Université libre) Collaboration in the Sn isotopic chain, where Hartree-Fock-Bogoliubov (HFB) calculations predict relatively deep and well-defined secondary PES minima. Specifically, a comprehensive set of lifetime data along the $^{114}$Sn - $^{120}$Sn chain had been acquired at the 9 MV Tandem facility of IFIN-HH, focusing on 0$^+$ excitations up to 0$_{4}$$^{+}$. These datasets form the core of G. Corbari PhD thesis [G. Corbari, PhD thesis, University of Milan (2025)].

Building upon this framework,  in P3 the Collaboration extended the investigation of shape coexistence to the Z = 48 Cd isotopes near the stability valley. For decades, cadmium nuclei have been regarded as textbook examples of nearly pure phonon excitations. However, recent experimental results for $^{110,112}$Cd reveal a much more complex picture, suggesting a rich pattern of shape coexistence involving at least the two or three lowest excited 0+ states. 

This perspective is reinforced by beyond-mean-field calculations, which associate the 0$_{1}^{+}$, 0$_{2}^{+}$, 0$_{3}^{+}$ and  0$_{4}^{+}$ states in $^{110,112}$Cd with nearly spherical, prolate, oblate, and prolate deformations, respectively. Consequently, an experiment aimed at populating excited 0$^{+}$ states in $^{110,112,114}$Cd nuclei was successfully carried out at IFIN-HH in June 2026. The measurements were performed using $+1n$ and $-1n$ transfer reactions induced by a $^{13}$C beam on thick $^{111,113}$Cd targets at sub-Coulomb-barrier energies. The choice of this approach was motivated by its demonstrated effectiveness in populating excited 0$^+$ states in the $^{116,118,120}$Sn isotopes in a previous experimental campaign (G. Corbari, PhD thesis, University of Milan (2025)).

For most of the excited 0$^{+}$ states under investigation, lifetimes have not yet been determined. The experimental data already accumulated using thick targets are expected to provide lifetime information in the range of approximately 0.2–1 ps. Importantly, however, several 0$^+$$\rightarrow$2$^{+}$ $\gamma$-ray transitions shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:WP2_IFIN_highlight} exhibit no noticeable Doppler-shifted components, indicating that the corresponding states may have significantly longer lifetimes, extending into the picosecond range. This observation provides a strong motivation for a dedicated experiment at IFIN-HH, employing the recoil-distance Doppler-shift (RDDS) technique with a plunger device to determine the lifetimes of excited 0$^+$ states in $^{110,112,114}$Cd.

At \textbf{INFN-LNL}, 16 excellent projects, using AGATA tracking array, were performed in P3. One of the highlights was the project aimed at production yields of neutron-rich heavy nuclei in the $^{238}$U+$^{124}$Sn multinucleon transfer reaction.
In July 2026, the PIAVE-ALPI accelerator complex provided for the very first time a $^{238}$U beam to LNL Users. The EURO-LABS experiment 25.032 exploited the full capabilities of the PRISMA-AGATA setup to study multinucleon transfer (MNT) reactions induced by a 1440 MeV $^{238}$U beam impinging on thin (300 $\mu$g/cm$^{2}$) $^{124}$Sn targets.
The main aim of the experiment was to measure the cross sections for the production of neutron-rich actinides in MNT reactions at energies slightly above the Coulomb barrier and the competition with transfer-induced fission as a function of the number of transferred neutrons and protons. This measurement was made possible by a unique combination of detectors: the PRISMA large-acceptance magnetic spectrometer for the identification of Sn-like ions in mass A, nuclear charge Z and Q value; the PRISMA second arm, composed of a MWPPAC and a Bragg chamber, for the detection of the corresponding U-like nuclei; the AGATA $\gamma$-ray tracking array for the detection of coincident $\gamma$ rays emitted by both target-like and beam-like ions. PRISMA was initially set at the grazing angle for target-like particles, 41$^o$, and the second arm at the kinematically correlated angle of 29$^o$. In the second part of the experiment, PRISMA was rotated to 29$^o$ to explore an angular range where deep-inelastic components of the reaction are expected to become more significant.


At \textbf{NLC-CCB} 6 projects were conducted in P3. The main objective of one measurement was testing the PRIDE detectors for range verification. The PRIDE project (Proton Range and Imaging Device) aims at the integration of a proton tomography (pCT) scanner and a range verification detector for proton therapy within the same device. An extensive work on pCT has been carried out by the Spanish collaboration at the Krakow CCB facility in previous periods. The objective of the experiment in P3, being a continuation of the first tests done at CCB (Dec-2022) and CMAM (Jun-2023), was to use the components of planned device for proton range verification, and exploring the possibility of acquiring information of the Bragg peak position through the detection of neutrons. 
The newly designed system (shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:WP2_CCB_Pride} ) exploited the detection of neutrons in transversal as well as in coaxial position to the proton beam. The transversal detection of neutrons is based on the use of a neutron-proton (n-p) converter, namely a pixelated plastic scintillator and two Double-sided Si Strip Detectors (DSSD) to track the protons produced in the former. The coaxial detection system being a pure LaCl3 crystal with excellent n-gamma separation, provided an information of the gamma and neutron counting rates, that can be related to the proximity of the Bragg peak. The main goal of the measurement was to test the system with proton beams from 70 to 160 MeV and phantoms of PMMA and water. A second goal was to measure the angular distribution of the neutrons produced in PMMA to serve as benchmark for Monte Carlo simulation codes. Both goals were successfully achieved.

At \textbf{NLC-SLCJ} 7 experiments were performed in P3. As the example of a highlight, the T-INSIDE experiment  was carried out to investigate the timing response of Silicon Carbide (SiC) detectors coupled to dedicated fast front-end electronics. This development is relevant to precise time-of-flight measurements for the identification and characterization of Radioactive Ion Beams, with particular attention to the requirements of the FRAISE (Fragment In-flight Separator) facility at INFN-LNS. The measurement employed the $^{12}$C+$^{12}$C reaction at a beam energy of 73 MeV and exploited the kinematic correlation between the two outgoing carbon nuclei. Two SiC detectors, each comprising 2x2 pixels with a total area of 1 cm$^2$ and a thickness of 100$\mu$, were installed inside the ICARE scattering chamber. By varying their laboratory angles, the timing response could be investigated at different deposited energies while keeping the incident beam energy fixed. 
The main experimental highlight is a preliminary timing resolution of approximately 124 ps for an individual pixel at a deposited energy of 34 MeV. This result was obtained with both detectors positioned at approximately 43$^o$, selecting inelastic scattering events in which one $^{12}$C nucleus remained in its ground state and the other was populated at an excitation energy of 4.44 MeV. Fig.~\ref{fig:WP2_SLCJ_result} shows the first obtained preliminary results.

For coincident events, the crossing time of each signal was determined at 30$\%$ of its maximum amplitude. A Gaussian fit to the distribution of the differences between the crossing times yielded a $\sigma$$_{tot}$$\approx$175 ps, 
corresponding to an individual resolution of approximately 124 ps under the assumption of equal and independent timing contributions and negligible additional broadening. 
This preliminary result of the resolution is well below the original goal of 250 ps for the T-INSIDE experiment, and supports the ongoing development of SiC detectors for precise timing applications. Calibration refinements, event-selection studies, and time-of-flight corrections are ongoing to consolidate the result.

\subparagraph{Task 2.2: Radioactive Ion Beams} \mbox{}

At \textbf{GSI/FAIR} experiments using FRS recorded a comprehensive dataset for projectile-fragmentation products (Z = 82 to 89) from $^{238}$U on a Be target, providing essential data to refine fragmentation models and support future NUSTAR experiments. 
ESR has been used for investigating the rare double-gamma decay mode in 0$^+$$\rightarrow$0$^+$ transitions (\url{https://doi.org/10.1002/piuz.202570106}); this study measured isolated double-photon decays in $^{72}$Ge, and compared the two-photon decay in $^{98}$Zr and $^{98}$Mo to assess whether enhanced transition rates depend on nuclear structure, and measures of de-excitation probabilities over a wide energy range in excited $^{238}$U and $^{239}$U. 
For the first time, simultaneous measurements of fission, gamma, and multi-neutron emission (up to three neutrons) were achieved in a storage ring setting. R3B setup has been used for commissioning of key detectors (CALIFA, Si-tracker FOOT, NeuLAND). Cross-section measurements for (p,pd) reactions on various carbon isotopes were performed indicating the presence of strongly correlated neutron-proton pairs, with quasi-deuteron behavior influencing nucleon “dressing” in the nuclear medium. 
DESPEC gathered spectroscopic data around N=126 shell closer, a critical region for r-process nucleosynthesis, where new level schemes and transition probabilities help benchmark models describing the interplay of single-particle orbitals and collective excitations in heavy nuclei relevant for the astrophysical sites. 
FRS Ion Catcher has been used in three different experiments. A proof-of-principle study using slowed-down uranium beams in a Cryogenic Stopping Cell (see Fig.~\ref{fig:WP2_GSI_CSC}) demonstrated that multi-nucleon transfer (MNT) reactions can produce neutron-rich isotopes (A $\approx$ 160–250). MNT products were successfully extracted and identified with the MR-TOF mass spectrometer, paving the way for future RIB production at Super-FRS (cf. \url{https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nuclphysa.2025.123041}, \url{https://doi.org/10.1140/epja/s10050-026-01914-4}).

Also focusing on exotic nuclei from Br to Rh, this study addresses issues such as isospin symmetry breaking, the Wigner effect, and unexpected mass discrepancies (e.g., $^{70}$Br). The FRS Ion Catcher, combined with a $^{107}$Ag fragmentation beam and SIS-18 accelerator mode, delivered precise mass measurements, essential for validating nuclear models and rp-process calculations. 
EXPERT detector, using a $^9$C beam, probes Thomas-Ehrman shifts in mirror pairs (e.g., $^5$H-$^5$Be, $^6$H-$^5$B, $^7$H-$^7$C) and measures decay energies, widths, and half-lives (down to picoseconds) via multi-particle angular correlations. Upgraded high-rate tracking detectors have enabled high-statistics data collection, allowing for improved measurements of nuclear state widths and the identification of novel multi-proton decay mechanisms.



At \textbf{JYFL}, among many projects realized in P3, a particular highlight is the successful experiment to perform a laser spectroscopic study of $^{94}$Ag, which goes alongside another study to determine the atomic mass of the same nucleus. The aim was to perform in-source laser spectroscopy on the high-spin (7$^{+}$ and 21$^{+}$) isomeric states of the self-conjugate neutron deficient N=Z silver isotope $^{94}$Ag (Z=47, N=47) at IGISOL to determine the charge radii and magnetic dipole moments of these states. A measurement on the 21$^{+}$ state would mark the first laser spectroscopy measurement of a proton-emitting state.
The experiment yielded a hyperfine spectrum for the 7$^{+}$ state and a preliminary spectrum for the 21$^{+}$ state. The 21$^{+}$ state needs to be revisited in the near future to gather more statistics. In addition, the hyperfine structure of $^{95}$Ag was measured with high statistics. The obtained data is under analysis and waiting for additional data to be obtained in the near future. Such studies of this N=Z silver isotope is usually found on the list of key experiments and challenges for all major accelerator facilities, and thus is a particularly high-impact result for the IGISOL facility, as JYFL.  One should note, however, that the analysis of complex data sets from the experiments (both radioactive and stable ion beam experiments) can take up to several years, thus the impact from EURO-LABS projects will come with a slight delay.


At \textbf{CERN/ISOLDE} several experiments addressed  in P3 very topical physics in exotic nuclei. Among them, below are chosen two main highlights achieved  in this period by ISOLDE user groups.

First one concerns nuclear structure and collectivity of neutron-rich tin isotopes around $^{132}$.
Tin (Z = 50) is the heaviest element with two doubly magic isotopes, $^{100}$Sn and $^{132}$Sn, and the region around $^{132}$Sn is important both for nuclear structure and for the astrophysical rapid neutron-capture process that forges heavy elements. Experiment IS702 at HIE-ISOLDE aimed to measure how collective the two even-even neighbours of $^{132}$Sn, $^{130}$Sn (N = 80) and $^{134}$Sn (N = 84), really are. The key quantity is the reduced transition probability B(E2; 0$^+$$\rightarrow$2$^+$), a sensitive measure of quadrupole collectivity. Unlike most doubly magic nuclei, $^{132}$Sn had been found to be unexpectedly collective, but its neighbours remained unmeasured and models disagreed. To settle this, the tin beams were extracted as SnS molecules to suppress isobaric contaminants, then charge-bred and post-accelerated to 4.4 MeV/u onto $^{206}$Pb and $^{194}$Pt targets, where Coulomb excitation populates the first 2$^+$ state. The decay $\gamma$ rays were recorded with the Miniball spectrometer in coincidence with the scattered 
particles. In the $^{134}$Sn experiment the main challenge was the beam composition, since the beam contained only about 7$\%$ of the isotope of interest, the rest being mostly $^{134}$Ba and $^{134}$Te.
The 2$_{1}$$^{+}$$\rightarrow$0$_{1}$$^{+}$ observed transitions in $^{130}$Sn and $^{134}$Sn (Fig.~\ref{fig:ISOLDE_1}a) led to reduced transition probabilities B(E2; 0$^{+}$$\rightarrow$2$^{+}$) = 0.049(8) e2b2 for $^{130}$Sn and 0.045(8) e2b2 for $^{134}$Sn. These values are clearly larger than earlier results and, together with the 
known value for $^{132}$Sn, confirm a local maximum of collectivity at the doubly magic nucleus (Fig.~\ref{fig:ISOLDE_1}b). The high statistics also allowed the first determination of the spectroscopic quadrupole moments of the 2$^{+}$ states for both $^{130}$Sn and $^{134}$Sn. Both are consistent with zero, showing that the nuclei around $^{132}$Sn are close to spherical. Of the models tested, only the Monte Carlo Shell Model reproduces the collectivity of both isotopes, while shell-model calculations with an inert $^{132}$Sn core clearly underestimate $^{134}$Sn, pointing to a larger neutron effective charge or missing core-polarisation effects. These results are a stringent benchmark for theory.

Second highlight comes from the mass measurement of the neutron-deficient $^{96}$Cd with ISOLTRAP.  Nuclear masses reveal how tightly nucleons are bound, and differences between neighbouring masses expose shell closures. The region around the doubly magic, self-conjugate $^{100}$Sn is of great interest both for nuclear structure and for the astrophysical rapid proton-capture process, but it is extremely hard to reach experimentally. Experiment IS745 at ISOLDE aimed to measure the masses of $^{96-98}$Cd with the multi-reflection time-of-flight (MR-ToF) mass spectrometer of ISOLTRAP, in order to quantify the strength of the N = 50 shell closure at Z = 48 and follow its evolution towards $^{100}$Sn. In an MR-ToF device, ions bounce between two electrostatic mirrors for thousands of reflections, so that a path of several kilometres is covered within tens of milliseconds, giving high mass resolving power and single-ion sensitivity. The 
main challenge was the tiny production of $^{96}$Cd, of the order of 10$^{-2}$ ions per $\mu$C of protons. Success required a combination of developments: a LaC$_{x}$ target with better temperature homogeneity, which raised the yield by about a factor of five; a very stable resonance laser ion source; temperature stabilisation of the spectrometer; an improved cooler-buncher; and a proton current raised from 2 to 2.5 $\mu$A for most of the run.
In the week-long run less than 50 ions of $^{96}$Cd were detected, the lowest yield ever explored at ISOLTRAP. Blocking one of the laser ionisation steps made the peak disappear, confirming it as cadmium (Fig.~\ref{fig:ISOLDE_2}a). The masses of the ground and isomeric states of $^{97}$Cd and of $^{98}$Cd were also improved, and the excitation energy of the 25/2$^{+}$ isomer in $^{97}$Cd was firmly established. The resulting one-neutron empirical shell gap grows towards N = 50 and $^{100}$Sn (Fig.~\ref{fig:ISOLDE_2}b), as expected for a doubly magic nucleus. This trend is reproduced qualitatively by ab initio and density-functional calculations, and the measurement provides the first direct experimental constraint on the N = 50 gap in the cadmium chain, a stringent benchmark for nuclear theory close to $^{100}$Sn.

In \textbf{CLEAR-CNA} 3 notable experiments were performed in P3, aiming mostly at testing novel neutron detectors. The main achievement was the in-beam characterization of an ultra-low background nRPC neutron detector. Such neutron detection is essential for both scientific research and a wide range of applications, including nuclear safety, nuclear medicine, neutron radiography, illicit material detection, soil moisture assessment, and flux monitoring in neutron capture and hadron therapy. 
Researchers at LIP-Coimbra are developing innovative neutron detectors based on resistive plate chambers (RPCs) coupled with a $^{10}$B-enriched neutron converter. This approach offers high detection efficiency, excellent spatial resolution, and outstanding time resolution. 

Epithermal neutrons (below 108 keV) were produced in the CNA neutron beam line using the reaction p+$^7$Li at 1912 keV. Rapid quasi-monoenergetic neutrons were also produced using the reaction d+d at 1.5 MeV. Both types of neutrons were detected with the nRCP detector. The detector’s gamma-ray discrimination capability, as well as its response to epithermal and fast neutrons under different poliethilene  moderator configurations, was characterized through measurements performed at  CNA (see Fig.~\ref{fig:WP2_USE_neutrons}). \subparagraph{Task 2.4: Theoretical Support} \mbox{}

The theoretical support offered at \textbf{ECT*} continued to cover a broad range of subjects in various workshops and schools, which were characterized, as always, by a high degree of interaction and collaboration among participants. The majority of workshops dealt with the new ideas and methods that are enabling rapid progress in nuclear physics and nuclear astrophysics. At the foundations of these fields, the next generation of {\it ab initio} methods for the many-body problem was developed thanks to emulators and novel algorithms. A boot camp on the application of these methods to few-body systems fostered collaboration with Latin America, including a new generation of researchers. Cross-fertilization with the atomic and condensed-matter communities took place in methods to overcome the sign problem that plagues simulations of many-fermion systems. The latest developments in the calculation of electroweak response were discussed, and progress was made in innovative technologies to improve the already stringent tests of nuclear charge and magnetization distributions in atomic spectroscopy. The reaction and structure communities established points where development is needed to bridge the two areas, such as one-body densities and level densities near thresholds. Advances were made in predicting, constraining, and measuring neutron-induced cross-sections of interest to astrophysics. In parallel, much progress was achieved in various workshops dedicated to the search for physics beyond the Standard Model (BSM) using nuclei. A Doctoral Training Program (DTP) trained PhD students from all over the world in the nuclear and particle theory that enables the analysis of the corresponding experiments. A workshop brought together the atomic, nuclear, and particle communities to discuss the steps needed to improve single beta decay and resolve the existing tension in the unitarity of the CKM matrix. Recommendations were made to reduce the uncertainties in the nuclear matrix elements for neutrinoless double beta decay and establish a centralized repository with results from different methods of calculation. The challenges in experiments where nuclei catalyze muon-to-electron conversion were discussed in view of the hadronic and nuclear methods needed to incorporate BSM physics. The nuclear and particle communities coordinated efforts to set experimental strategies to decipher the X17 anomaly seen in certain nuclei decays, for which unusual BSM interpretations have been put forward. 
Other workshops and DTP were dedicated to general tools common to theorists and experimentalists. A workshop brought to young researchers the latest developments in quantum science, with speakers from not only academy but also quantum-technology companies. A DTP offered an in-depth exploration of superconducting devices, which are poised to play a central role in the near future of quantum sensing and computing. In a workshop, the use of artificial intelligence, machine learning, and digital-twin technology was explored across the entire life cycle of nuclear and particle experiments. Finally, substantial advances were demonstrated in statistical emulation, correlated likelihood development, and data-driven forecasting for both nuclear theory and experiment.

RP3 also brought to completion the three Theo4Exp platforms: MeanField4Exp at {\bf IFJ-PAN}, Reaction4Exp at {\bf USE}, and Structure4Exp at {\bf UMIL}. These platforms have continued to offer VA to integrated tools developed by theorists to help the design and analysis of nuclear experiments. To facilitate their use, as well as to receive user feedback, a hands-on workshop was organized at ECT*. MeanField4Exp has made available to users a combination of methods from nuclear structure theory and quantum mechanics with advanced techniques from applied mathematics for optimizing Hamiltonian parameters, analysing shape transitions, and addressing nuclear symmetries. Reaction4Exp calculates various types of scattering of interest to contemporary experiments, such as elastic and inelastic scattering, Coulomb break-up, and transfer reactions. Structure4Exp makes available computer codes for self-consistent and shell-model calculations of nuclear ground and excited states. 


In summary, the scientific output generated under WP2 demonstrates substantial advancements in nuclear physics, underscoring the vital role of cross-facility collaboration between theorists and experimentalists.
